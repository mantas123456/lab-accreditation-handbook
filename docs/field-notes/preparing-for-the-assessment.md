# Preparing for the on-site assessment

<p class="clause-badge clause-badge--draft">Field note · Draft</p>

*Draft: written, not yet reviewed by a second practitioner.*

The on-site assessment is where the assessors check that the system you
described on paper is the one the lab actually runs. Most of the work that
decides the outcome happens months earlier (see the
[accreditation journey](../start-here/journey.md)). But the last two weeks,
and how the day itself is run, still make a real difference. This page is
about those.

## The two weeks before

**Confirm the plan.** The lead assessor normally sends an assessment plan:
who comes, when, which clauses and which methods. Check it against your
staff rota and test schedule. If a method will be witnessed, make sure
there are real samples to test and an authorised analyst on site that day.

**Do not change the system now.** Two weeks is too short to introduce a new
procedure and have records showing it works. A fresh procedure with no
records behind it invites the question "how did you do this before?". Fix
small errors in documents through normal document control
([8.3](../management/8-3-document-control.md)); leave bigger changes for
after the visit and note them as planned improvements.

**Close what you can.** Look at your action list: open corrective actions
([8.7](../management/8-7-corrective-action.md)), overdue calibrations
([6.4](../resources/6-4-equipment.md)), unreviewed QC charts
([7.7](../process/7-7-validity-of-results.md)). An item that is open, with a
plan and a date, is acceptable. An item that is overdue and forgotten is a
finding.

**Trace a few reports yourself.** Pick three recent reports, one per main
method, and follow each back: contract review, sample receipt, raw data,
equipment used, calibration status on that day, QC result for that batch,
the analyst's authorisation, the person who released the report. This is
what the assessor will do (a "vertical audit"). If a link is missing, you
would rather find it now.

**Brief the staff.** Remind everyone who the assessors are, when they will
be in which room, and that it is fine to say "I don't know, but I know
where to find it". Do not rehearse answers. Assessors notice scripted
replies, and they test understanding, not memory.

**Prepare the practical things.** A quiet room with a table and power; a
list of where records are kept; read access to any electronic systems;
copies of the scope, the manual, and the documents index. The
[pre-assessment readiness checklist](../downloads/pre-assessment-checklist.docx)
has an "On the day" section for this.

## The day itself

**Opening meeting.** The lead assessor confirms the scope, the plan and the
rules for the day. Have top management there; it shows that the system is
theirs, not only the quality manager's. Ask about anything in the plan you
are unsure of.

**One guide per assessor.** Give each assessor a staff member who knows
where things are and can fetch records quickly. The guide should not answer
on behalf of the analyst being interviewed.

**Witnessed tests.** The analyst works exactly as on any other day, with
the method and the forms they always use. If something goes wrong (a QC
result fails, a balance check is out), follow your own procedure in front
of the assessor. Handling a failure correctly is strong evidence that the
system works.

**Keep notes.** Someone on the lab side should write down each question
that needed a record you could not produce, and every point the assessor
says they will raise. It saves surprises at the closing meeting.

**Closing meeting.** The assessors present their findings. Listen, ask
questions to make sure you understand each one, and correct facts if a
finding rests on a misunderstanding (for example, a record that exists but
was not found in time). Do not argue the grading on the day. Check the
deadlines you are given before everyone leaves.

## Answering assessors' questions

- **Answer the question asked**, then stop. Long answers open new topics.
- **Show, don't tell.** "Here is the record" beats "we always do that".
- **Say when you don't know**, and say where the answer is: "The QC rules
  are in section 6 of the method; I'll show you."
- **Explain in your own words.** An analyst who can say why the control
  sample matters, and what they do when it fails, is convincing. One who
  recites the procedure is less so.
- **Do not guess.** A wrong confident answer is worse than "let me check".
- **Disagree politely, with evidence.** If you think an assessor has
  misread a requirement, ask which clause they mean and show your record.

## A made-up example

At a small environmental lab, the assessor asked an analyst how she knew
the oven was at the right temperature. She opened the equipment record,
showed the weekly thermometer check, and pointed to one week where the
reading had been out and the note that followed: results held, oven
adjusted, retests done. The assessor moved on in two minutes. The same
question at another lab produced "it's calibrated every year", no record of
checks in between, and a finding against [6.4](../resources/6-4-equipment.md).

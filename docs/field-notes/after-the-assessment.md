# After the assessment

<p class="clause-badge clause-badge--draft">Field note · Draft</p>

*Draft: written, not yet reviewed by a second practitioner.*

The assessors have left and you have a list of findings. Almost every lab
gets some, including labs that are accredited in the end. What decides the
outcome now is how well you respond: whether each fix is real, whether you
found the cause, and whether you can prove it. This page walks through
that, with a made-up example.

## First, read the findings carefully

Within a day or two, sit down with the people involved and go through the
written report line by line.

- **Understand each finding.** What exactly was seen, where, and which
  clause it relates to. If something is unclear, ask the lead assessor now,
  not at the deadline.
- **Note the grade.** Bodies grade findings differently (the names vary),
  and the grade usually sets how quickly you must respond and whether the
  assessors need to visit again. Your body's procedures explain the grades.
- **Write down the deadlines.** There is often one deadline for sending a
  plan, and a later one for sending evidence that the actions are done.
  Put both in your action list with an owner. A missed deadline can delay
  the decision on accreditation, however good the fix.
- **Treat each finding as a nonconformity** in your own system
  ([7.10](../process/7-10-nonconforming-work.md),
  [8.7](../management/8-7-corrective-action.md)), with its own record.

## Three parts to every response

A good response has three parts. Assessors look for all of them.

1. **Correction**: fix the specific thing that was found. The overdue
   calibration is done; the missing signature is added with today's date
   and a note.
2. **Root cause**: why it was possible. Not "who forgot" but "what allowed
   it to be forgotten".
3. **Corrective action**: change something so the cause is removed, and
   check other places where the same cause could act.

A response with only a correction is the most common reason a finding is
sent back. It is also listed as a common finding in
[8.7](../management/8-7-corrective-action.md).

## Finding the root cause

Keep it short and honest. Two simple methods work for most findings:

- **Ask "why?" until the answer is something you can change.** Usually
  three to five times. Stop when you reach a process, a resource or a
  decision, not a person.
- **Ask "where else?"** If one method had no uncertainty budget, check all
  the others. If one balance missed a check, check every instrument on the
  same schedule. Include what you found in the response, even if the answer
  is "nowhere else".

If the honest root cause is "we did not know this was required", say so,
and say how you will make sure you hear about such requirements in future.
That is more convincing than inventing a cause.

## Evidence of closure

The body decides on evidence, not on promises. For each finding, send:

- the record of the correction (a certificate, a corrected form, an
  amended report);
- the root cause analysis, in a few lines;
- the corrective action and proof that it is in place: a revised
  procedure with its new version number, a training record, an updated
  schedule;
- where relevant, the result of the "where else?" check;
- where needed, a short plan to check later that the action worked, with a
  date. Some actions can only be shown to work after months of data.

Name files clearly by finding number. One tidy folder per finding saves the
assessor time, and saves you a round of questions.

## A made-up example

A fuel-testing lab received this finding: *"The QC chart for sulfur content
had no record of review between March and June."*

- **Correction:** the quality manager reviewed the four months of data the
  same week, found no rule broken, and signed the review.
- **Root cause:** the review was a task for "the analyst" in the method,
  with no named person and no frequency. When the usual analyst moved to
  another method in March, nobody picked it up.
- **Corrective action:** the method now names the role responsible and a
  monthly review, and the review was added to the lab's action list with a
  reminder. The quality manager checked the other eight QC charts; two had
  the same wording and were changed too.
- **Evidence sent:** the signed review, the revised method (version 4),
  the two other revised methods, and the action list entry.
- **Effectiveness check:** the next internal audit
  ([8.8](../management/8-8-internal-audits.md)) will look at three months of
  review records for all QC charts.

The finding was closed without further questions.

## Do not lose the lessons

After the decision, add the findings to the inputs of your next
[management review](../management/8-9-management-review.md), check whether
your [risk register](../management/8-5-risks-and-opportunities.md) needs an
update, and plan the next internal audit to look at the areas that were
found weak. Surveillance assessors often start with last time's findings.

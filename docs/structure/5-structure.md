# 5 Legal entity, scope, roles and authority

## In one sentence

Be clear about who legally owns the lab's work, exactly what activities you
claim to do under the standard, and who in the lab is responsible for what,
including who stands in when they are away.

## What an assessor looks for

- **A legal entity** (or a defined part of one) that is legally
  responsible for the lab's activities.
- **Named management** with overall responsibility for the lab.
- **A defined scope**: the activities you perform under the standard,
  documented, and no claims of accreditation for anything outside it. That
  includes work done on customer sites or in temporary facilities.
- **The lab's place in a larger organisation**, shown in a chart, with
  the relationships between the lab, its parent and any related bodies.
- **Responsibilities and authority** written down for everyone whose work
  affects results: management, technical staff, quality staff,
  verification and support roles.
- **People with the authority and resources** to keep the management
  system running and to spot and act on departures from it.
- **Communication**: staff know how their work matters to results and to
  the management system.
- **Changes managed** so the system keeps working when people, premises or
  scope change.

## How to do it in a small lab

1. **Draw the organisation chart** of the lab and, if it is part of
   something bigger, of how it connects to the parent. Show who reports to
   whom, and where the quality role sits.
2. **Write short role descriptions** for each position, with
   responsibilities and authority: who can authorise reports, who can stop
   work, who approves methods, who signs purchase orders for critical
   supplies.
3. **Name deputies** for every key role. In a small lab, absence is the
   biggest risk to the system.
4. **Keep the scope precise**: test, material, method and range. Use the
   same wording as your accreditation certificate.
5. **Plan changes.** Before a key person leaves, a method moves or a new
   site opens, check what the change affects and who must be told,
   including your accreditation body.

## Worked example

A made-up biofuel lab with five staff sits inside a research institute.

**Organisation.** The institute is the legal entity. The lab head reports to
the institute director. The quality manager reports to the lab head on
daily matters but has direct access to the director on impartiality or
quality concerns.

**Roles and deputies matrix:**

| Role | Holder | Deputy | Key authorities |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lab head | A | Technical manager | Overall responsibility; approves budget and staffing |
| Technical manager | B | Senior analyst C | Approves methods and validations; authorises reports; stops and restarts work |
| Quality manager | D | Lab head | Runs audits and document control; opens corrective actions; reports on the system |
| Senior analyst | C | Analyst E | Tests; checks results; trains new analysts |
| Analyst | E | Senior analyst C | Tests within authorised methods |

**Scope extract** (as on the certificate): "Solid biofuels: moisture
content, ash content, gross and net calorific value, carbon, hydrogen,
nitrogen and sulphur, by the named standard methods."

**Change.** When the technical manager goes on six months' leave, the lab
head confirms in writing that C acts in that role, checks C's
authorisation records, and informs the accreditation body, as its rules
require for key staff changes.

## Common findings

- **Role descriptions that do not match reality**, or missing for some
  staff.
- **No deputies**, so reports are authorised by someone without the
  authority when the usual person is away.
- **Accreditation claimed outside the scope**, for example on reports that
  include unaccredited tests without marking them.
- **The quality manager without real authority** or access to top
  management.
- **Key changes not reported** to the accreditation body.
